About this project

Following a request around the world

When you open a website, something physical happens: a signal leaves your device, crosses radio, glass and ocean, and arrives at a machine in a building somewhere else on Earth. This site turns that invisible journey into something you can watch, step by step, on a globe.

What you are looking at

You choose two points — where a request starts, and the data centre it is heading for — and the globe builds a route between them. Scrolling moves the request one hop at a time, and each hop explains the piece of infrastructure it is passing through: the tower that picks up your phone's radio signal, the fibre that carries it inland, the exchange points where separate networks hand traffic to each other, the submarine cables that cross oceans, and the data centre at the far end. A second path, in blue, traces the reply coming back.

With Starlink switched on the route takes a different shape. The request climbs to a satellite in low Earth orbit, may hop between satellites, and comes back down at a ground station before rejoining the ordinary terrestrial network — which is the part people are usually most surprised by.

Where the data comes from

The geography is real, and it is bundled with the site rather than fetched live, so the same thing renders the same way every time.

  • Satellites — orbital elements from CelesTrak's public catalogue, propagated in the browser. The dated snapshot and satellite count are shown on the globe page.
  • Coastlines, cities and place names — Natural Earth (public domain).
  • Submarine cables — TeleGeography's public cable map dataset.
  • Data centres — sites tagged in OpenStreetMap, under the ODbL.
  • Mobile coverage — a rasterised OpenCelliD snapshot published by the World Bank, sampled so that populated regions everywhere are represented rather than only dense city centres.
  • Starlink availability and ground stations — SpaceX's published coverage information.

What it is not

This is an educational representation, not a measurement. Nothing here is a live packet capture, and the route you see is not the exact path a specific request took.

  • Routes are plausible, not observed. They are built from real infrastructure — real cable landings, real facilities, real orbits — but a real request is decided hop by hop by routing protocols, and may take a different path each time.
  • Hops are summarised. A real traceroute crosses many more routers than the handful of stages shown. The stages stand for the major changes in the kind of infrastructure being used, which is the thing worth understanding first.
  • Satellite motion is sped up. Orbits run at roughly ten times real time so the movement is visible, and are propagated with a simplified orbital model rather than a full-precision one.
  • Coverage is modelled. A green marker means the simplified model treats that area as connected. It is not a guarantee that a particular network reaches a particular address.

Why bother visualising it

The internet is usually described in metaphors — the cloud, the web, a series of tubes — that quietly suggest it has no location. It does. Latency is distance. An ocean is a delay. A request from a remote town travels differently from one sent in a capital city, and the reason is physical infrastructure that mostly exists in places nobody thinks about.

Seeing a request move across a globe makes those relationships concrete in a way a diagram does not: why distant servers feel slower, why undersea cables matter, what a satellite link actually buys you, and what a traceroute is really telling you when it prints a list of names.
